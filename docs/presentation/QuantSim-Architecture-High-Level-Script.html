QuantSim Architecture — The High-Level View

A three-minute account of how QuantSim is put together: eight modules, each built as a modular monolith on the inside and deployed as an independent service on the outside, joined by one front door and one event backbone. This is the talk you give before anyone asks about a table or a topic name.

Opening · the claim

One sentence, then the diagram

Point at nothing yet — let them read the shape while you talk

Voiceover

QuantSim simulates markets, replays strategies against them, and scores the resulting portfolio for risk three different ways. That workload is not one shape. Some of it is a user clicking a button and waiting; some of it is an hour of persistent homology on a CPU node. So the architecture's whole job is to stop those two kinds of work from interfering with each other. Eight modules, eight services, one front door, one event log.

Tiers 1 and 2 · client and edge

The browser knows one address

Point at the browser box, then run your finger up the gateway column

Voiceover

Tier one is a single-page app with no business logic and no database access. What matters is that it talks to exactly one origin — the browser never addresses a service directly, so we can split or merge services without ever shipping a frontend release.

Tier two is the gateway, the only component with a public address. It terminates TLS, verifies the token, rate-limits, and routes each path prefix to the owning service. What it deliberately does not do is authorise. Authentication happens once at the edge; whether this user may run that particular action is decided inside the service that owns the action, because a gateway-only check would implicitly trust anything arriving by another route.

If asked “why not expose the services directly?” — that is eight public surfaces, eight TLS configs, eight auth implementations, and CORS sprawl.

Tier 3 · the eight modules

Why these eight and not some other eight

Point at the service stack — one sweep down it, M0 to M7

Voiceover

Tier three is eight autonomous services, and the split is by bounded context, not by technical layer. Platform owns identity and audit. Market Data owns instruments, prices, news, and derived series like rolling correlation. Simulation owns calibration and Monte Carlo. Trading owns the ordered log of what a strategy actually did. ML owns the model registry and the feature store. Risk and TDA owns stress scenarios and the topology pipeline. Explainability turns those outputs into attributions and plain English. Dashboard owns every widget, every alert rule, and every open browser connection.

The test each boundary had to pass was simple: can this thing change its data model without asking anyone's permission? Risk and TDA is the clearest yes. Persistent homology needs Ripser and GUDHI — heavyweight native libraries nothing else in the platform wants — and it is the most research-volatile code we have. As its own service it ships its own image and redeploys mid-experiment without touching trade execution.

The style decision

Modular monolith inside, microservice outside

Point at any single service box — and say “all eight look like this”

Voiceover

Here is the part people usually ask about, so I will get ahead of it. We are three people running eight services, and eight services built eight different ways would be unmaintainable. So every service is generated from one shared template and differs only in its domain layer. The template fixes the internal layering, the framework, the health probes, structured logging, tracing, the outbox and its drain, the resilient outbound client, the Kafka consumer wrapper, the RBAC dependency, and the migration setup.

That is the modular monolith half of the answer: inside any one of these boxes is a clean four-layer application, and the layering is identical in all eight. Microservices is what happens at the boundary — separate image, separate database, separate pipeline, separate scaling policy. Anyone who has worked in one service can navigate any other, and a cross-cutting fix is made once instead of eight times.

This beat is your defence against “isn't this over-engineered for three people?” — answer it before it is asked.

Tier 4 · integration

Kafka sits beside the request path, not under it

Point at the amber rail — then trace one solid teal arrow to show what does not touch it

Voiceover

Tier four is Kafka, and its position on this diagram is a deliberate correction. An earlier draft placed the event backbone as a layer between the domain and the data-access layer — every request passing through a log on the way to its own database. That makes writes slower and reads indirect, and it means a broker outage stops a service reading data it already owns.

So Kafka sits to the side. It carries three families of traffic: high-volume streams like ticks and order events, coarse domain facts other services react to, and the feeds that keep local read models current. Simulation does not know Dashboard exists — it announces that a run completed, and Dashboard reacts. Delivery is at-least-once, which means every consumer in the system is idempotent. That is not an optimisation; it is a precondition.

Tier 5 · data

Eight databases, and the bill that comes with them

Point at the column of database chips — one sweep, top to bottom

Voiceover

Each service owns one database and holds credentials to no other. This is the decision that actually separates microservices from a modular monolith, and it is the expensive one. There are no cross-service joins and no distributed transactions. Any view spanning two services is either assembled by the caller or maintained as a local projection, and consistency across the eight is eventual rather than transactional. We are not arguing that cost away — the low-level talk is largely about the machinery that makes it survivable.

Locally these are eight logical databases in one Postgres container, each with its own role — so ownership is enforced by privilege, not by good manners.

Close

What the shape buys

Point at nothing — look at the room

Voiceover

So: a change to the TDA pipeline cannot break trade execution. The three compute-heavy services scale, and fail, independently of the interactive path. A fault degrades one capability instead of the whole application. Eight modules, nine releasable units, sixteen running deployments — and one shared template that keeps all of it learnable by three people.

Likely first questions
  • “Why not a modular monolith end to end?” It was version 0.1 of the design. It lost on independent scaling of the three compute-heavy services, and on the native-dependency isolation Risk/TDA needs.
  • “Isn't eight databases overkill?” Eight schemas in one instance leaves every service one convenient JOIN from a distributed monolith, and shares a failure domain besides.
  • “Where is the service mesh?” Deliberately absent. Retries, breakers and mTLS live in the shared template, which is sufficient at eight services and avoids a large debugging burden.
  • “What happens when Kafka is down?” Reads keep working — they never touch it. Writes commit locally and queue in the outbox; the platform catches up when the broker returns.
