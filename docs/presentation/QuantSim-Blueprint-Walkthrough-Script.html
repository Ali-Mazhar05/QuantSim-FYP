0:000:25
Opening

Start with the question, not the system

Diagram 2Far left — the person and speech bubble, then the front desk. Point at the bubble on your first sentence.
Voiceover

Everything QuantSim does exists to answer one question: is this portfolio risky, and if so, why? That sounds simple. It is not, because there is no single number sitting anywhere that tells you.

So think of the system as a building. You walk in with your question. The front desk checks your badge and sends you to exactly one room — you never wander the corridors yourself, and you never open a filing cabinet yourself.

Do not say “API gateway” yet. You earn that word at 2:05, once they already know what the thing does.
0:250:40
The top row

Rooms one to four — building the picture

Diagram 2Top row, left to right. Touch each numbered badge as you name it: 1 archive, 2 what-if room, 3 trading floor, 4 forecaster.
Voiceover

Room one is the archive. Every price and every headline we hold, cleaned and checked before it is filed — because once a bad number is in the building, everyone downstream has already copied it.

Room two is the what-if room. It takes real history and generates thousands of plausible futures from it, so we are not betting on the one path that actually happened.

Room three is the trading floor. It plays your strategy through those futures, in strict order, and records every buy and sell it would have made.

Room four is the forecaster. It has learned from past behaviour, so it can tell you the odds — how volatile things are about to get, what regime we are in.

Four rooms in forty seconds is ten seconds each. If you feel yourself elaborating, you are already over — cut to the next badge.
1:050:35
The bottom row

Rooms five to seven — and the answer comes back

Diagram 2Bottom row, right to left: 5 stress lab, 6 translator, 7 control room. Then follow the long teal arrow all the way back to the screen on the far left.
Voiceover

Room five is the stress lab, and it is the one that makes this a risk product rather than a reporting tool. It breaks your portfolio on purpose — a flash crash, a liquidity shock, a whole sector collapsing — and measures what would actually survive.

Room six is the translator. It takes all of that and says it in one sentence you can act on, with the reasons attached.

Room seven is the control room. It is watching all of it, and when something crosses a line it raises the alarm. Notice the arrow: you never refresh anything. The answer comes back to your screen on its own.

1:400:25
The two rules

Cabinets and the mail room

Diagram 2Point at any one filing cabinet, then the legend line under it, then the purple Internal mail band along the bottom.
Voiceover

Two rules govern the whole building, and they are the entire architecture in plain language.

First, every room keeps its own files, and no room can open another room's cabinet. If you want something from another room, you ask that room — you do not reach into its drawers.

Second, the mail room. Rooms leave notes for each other rather than standing in each other's doorways waiting. The stress lab does not wait for the forecaster to be free; it gets a note when the forecaster is done.

These two sentences are the payload of the whole high-level talk. Everything before was setup and everything after is vocabulary. Slow down here.

The switch

Same picture, engineering names

Diagram 1Scroll up to the poster. Run your finger down the numbered tier keys on the far left: 1, 2, 3, 5 — then across to the purple rail for 4.
Voiceover

Here is the same building with the real names on it. The person is tier one, the browser. The front desk is tier two, the API gateway — the only thing in the system with a public address. The seven rooms plus a security office are tier three: eight services, each owning one job. The filing cabinets are tier five: eight separate databases, one per service. And the mail room is tier four, Kafka.

One thing worth pointing out. Kafka is drawn as a rail beside the services, not as a band underneath them. That is deliberate. Nothing you sit and wait for goes through it — it carries notes between rooms, it is never on the path from a room to its own cabinet.

Why bother splitting it at all

Diagram 1Point at the amber Services band once, then stop pointing and look at the room.
Voiceover

The obvious question is why not build one program. Because the work is not one shape. Some of it is a person clicking a button and waiting two seconds. Some of it is an hour of heavy mathematics on a dedicated machine. Separating them means the slow work never makes the fast work feel slow, and a change to the risk mathematics cannot break trade execution. Eight modules, nine releasable pieces, sixteen things running — all built from one shared template so three people can actually maintain it.

Likely first questions
  • “Is this over-engineered for a student project?” The compute-heavy rooms need their own machines and their own libraries. The template is what keeps eight services readable by three people — a cross-cutting fix is made once, not eight times.
  • “What if one room goes down?” One capability degrades, not the building. Every call names in advance what it does when its dependency is unavailable.
  • “Is it real or simulated?” Be straight: the POC is a click-through with mocked data. The blueprint is the design being defended, not the code that exists today.

0:000:20
Tiers 1 and 2

One origin, one front door, and a boundary people get wrong

Diagram 1Blue band, then green band. Point at the red-flagged note at the bottom of the green band on your last sentence.
Voiceover

Tier one holds no business logic and no database access, and it speaks to exactly one origin — so the decomposition is invisible to the browser and can change without a frontend release.

Tier two is the gateway. TLS, token verification, rate limiting, routing by path prefix. The line worth stating explicitly is the one it does not cross: it authenticates, but it does not authorise. Whether this user may run this specific action is decided inside the service that owns the action — because a gateway-only check means anything reaching a service by another route is implicitly trusted.

0:200:45
Tier 3

Eight cells — and how to read the tags

Diagram 1Amber band. Point at one cell — M2 Simulation is the clearest — and read its two tags aloud. Then sweep the other seven without naming them individually.
Voiceover

Tier three is eight autonomous services, split by bounded context rather than by technical layer. Read one cell and you can read them all. Take Simulation: a module number, what it owns in one line, then two tags. The grey tag is its database — simulation_db, and it holds credentials to no other. The amber tag is its worker — a separate deployment with its own resource limits, which is where the heavy work actually runs.

Seven of the eight carry a worker tag. Only Platform is API-only. And the workers are not all the same animal — four are job workers that scale on queue backlog, three are continuous consumers that scale on partition count. Trading is the one with a correctness constraint attached: it scales by adding partitions, never by adding concurrency inside one, because its whole guarantee is per-portfolio ordering.

The test every boundary had to pass: can this thing change its data model without asking anyone's permission? Risk and TDA is the clearest yes — persistent homology needs Ripser and GUDHI, heavyweight native libraries nothing else wants, and it is the most volatile code we have.

If you are running long, cut the third paragraph. The tag-reading paragraph is the one that makes the rest of the poster legible.
1:050:30
Tier 4

Why the backbone is a rail and not a band

Diagram 1The purple rail on the right of the amber band. Point at its three bullets, then at the bold line at its foot.
Voiceover

Tier four carries three families of traffic. High-volume streams — ticks and order lifecycle, ordered per partition key. Coarse domain events other services react to. And the feeds that keep local read models current.

Its position is the argument. An earlier draft put the event backbone as a layer between the domain and the data-access layer, so every request traversed a log on the way to its own database. That makes writes slower, reads indirect, and a broker outage stops a service reading data it already owns. So it is drawn beside the request path. Delivery is at-least-once, which makes every consumer in the system idempotent — not as an optimisation, as a precondition.

This is the beat where an examiner who has seen the old six-layer diagram will interrupt. Good — you have just pre-answered them.
1:350:30
Tier 5

Hot, warm, cold — and the bill

Diagram 1Grey band, three cells left to right. Then point back up at the column of grey db tags in the amber band.
Voiceover

Three storage tiers, each partitioned by owning service. Hot is Postgres with TimescaleDB — eight databases, one per service. Warm is Redis, one logical database each, holding cache, job state and the task queue. Cold is Parquet on object storage, one system but per-service prefixes and credentials, for closed path sets and model artifacts.

Database-per-service is the decision that actually separates this from a modular monolith, and it is the expensive one. No cross-service joins, no distributed transactions. Any view spanning two services is assembled by the caller or maintained as a local projection, and consistency is eventual. We are not arguing that cost away — we are naming the machinery that makes it survivable.

Locally these are eight logical databases in one container, each with its own role — so the ownership rule is enforced by grant, not by good manners.
2:050:35
Right-hand panel

One run, eight steps, and the one that matters

Diagram 1Right column, Example flow panel. Walk the eight icons with the pointer. Land hard on step 7.
Voiceover

Concretely. A quant clicks run. The gateway verifies and routes to Simulation. Simulation validates, writes a job row marked queued, and hands back a job id — the request is over in milliseconds. The id goes onto its own Redis queue. A worker on a separate deployment claims it under a lease, splits the paths across its cores, and bulk-writes results.

Step seven is the one to dwell on. The worker does not publish to Kafka directly. It writes its business rows and an outbox row in one local transaction — both or neither — and a drain worker publishes from that outbox afterwards. That closes the gap where a service commits and then dies before announcing it, which would leave the databases permanently diverged with nothing to detect it. A crash now produces a duplicate, never a loss. Then Dashboard projects the event and pushes to the browser.

Simulation never talks to the browser. It announces facts; Dashboard owns the connection. That is why a Simulation redeploy drops no live client.

The stack, and the thing that makes eight survivable

Diagram 1Bottom right, the Technology stack table. Point at the “Scaffolding” row specifically.
Voiceover

Python and FastAPI throughout, so the service code and the quant code are the same language with no rewrite across a boundary. Kafka in every environment, including development, so ordering and lag bugs surface on a laptop rather than in production. Postgres and TimescaleDB hot, Redis warm, Parquet cold.

And the row I would point at if I could only point at one: scaffolding. One shared template. Resilience policy, tracing, the outbox, migrations, the RBAC dependency — written once, not eight times. That single row is the reason a three-person team can run eight services at all.

Likely first questions
  • “Why not put jobs on Kafka too?” Job semantics — claim, lease, retry, dead-letter, one consumer — and log semantics — durable ordered replay, many consumers, offsets — are different problems. Collapsing them loses one of the two.
  • “What happens when Kafka is down?” Reads keep working, because they never touch it. Writes commit locally and queue in the outbox; the platform catches up when the broker returns.
  • “Where is the service mesh?” Deliberately absent. Retries, breakers and mTLS live in the template, which is sufficient at eight services and avoids a large debugging burden. It is recorded as an open question, not a settled no.
  • “Two services need the same data — who owns it?” One owner, always. Rolling correlation lives in Market Data, not Risk, because a correlation matrix is derived market data by definition — and two implementations of one rolling window would eventually disagree.
